# Call prep brief

The best calls start from what the prospect said, not from what we want to pitch. A one-page brief keeps the focus on their situation and makes the desired next step explicit before the call starts.

## Inputs
Ask only for what is missing:
- Company, attendee names and roles, call date and length
- The goal of the call (discovery, demo, negotiation, partnership)
- Prior notes or email history, if any

## Steps
1. Search the web for the company and each attendee: role, tenure, public statements, recent news. Record sources.
2. Read any prior notes or threads the user provides. Separate what the prospect said from what we assumed, because assumptions presented as facts steer the call wrong.
3. Build the brief in the format below, on one page.
4. Flag what is unverified or inferred.

## Format
**Call:** [Company], [date], [length], goal in one line

- **Who is on the call:** name, role, one relevant fact each
- **Company snapshot:** 3 bullets (what they do, size or stage if public, one recent event with source)
- **What we know they want:** in their words, from prior notes. Write "unknown" if there are none.
- **Hypotheses to test:** 2–3 things we think are true but haven't confirmed
- **Questions to ask:** 5–7 open questions in order, starting with their situation before our offer
- **Likely objections:** 2–3, each with a short, honest response
- **Desired outcome:** the specific next step to ask for before hanging up
- **Do not raise:** topics to avoid, for example pricing before discovery

Bullets are right here because the reader scans the page just before the call.

## Rules, and why
- **Only sourced or user-provided facts, and mark inference as inference.** A wrong fact said confidently on a call costs more than a gap in the brief.
- **No invented stats, funding rounds or quotes.**
- **Professional context only for attendees.** Skip personal or private information; it isn't needed and can feel intrusive.
- **One page.** A longer brief won't be read before the call.
